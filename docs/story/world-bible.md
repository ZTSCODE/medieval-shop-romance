## 0. Register: between plain and strange

The target is a world that feels **written**, not generated: as dense and specific as real history, and never odd just to be odd. Two failures to avoid:

| Too plain (口水) | Too strange (为怪而怪) | Aim |
|---|---|---|
| magic licence | the Leafbind Accord | **the Assize of Rings** |
| magic police | Lampwardens of the Ledger | **riders of the Assize**; on the road people just say **Wardens** |
| wizard | Leaflifter | **maker** (the Lee); **ringed man** (the street); **practitioner** (the law) |
| magic forest | the Unwritten Wood | **Blackthorn Wood**; in the Lee, just **the Thorn** |
| fairies | the Sídhe-Folk of Annwn | **the Good People**, or simply **Themselves** |

Rules:

1. **Use real words with real weight.** Medieval law, craft, church and folklore already have a rich vocabulary: assize, reeve, tithe, wake, hedge-maker, holy well, guest-right, the Good People. The reader half-knows these words, so they feel solid.
2. **Invent only proper names**, and draw each culture's names from one coherent source: the Crown's names are Latinate; the Lee's are Gaelic.
3. **Everything has three names.** The law, the street and the Lee each call the same thing something different. Which word a character uses tells you where they stand.
4. **Prefer the concrete noun.** Write "a farrier's file", not "a tool"; "oatcake and buttermilk", not "food".
5. **The prose models** are Ursula K. Le Guin's *Earthsea*, Susanna Clarke's *Jonathan Strange & Mr Norrell*, and Guy Gavriel Kay for magic worlds built on real history; Mary Renault and E. M. Forster for restraint between men. Write short declarative sentences, with one image per beat and room for silence.

## 1. The land

The realm has two names. In the old tongue it is **Ardmor**, "the great height", a real Irish place-name pattern (compare Ardmore). The Crown's documents use the Latin form, **Ardmoria**, and the Crown's officers say *the Province*, as though it were still newly conquered. It is a country of wet hills, hedged fields, slate and limestone, and old woods that the roads go around.

Over the sea to the south lies the **Crown's homeland**. No one in Ardmor bothers to name it, much as Irish peasants rarely named the English court.

## 2. History in layers

| When | What happened | What it left behind |
|---|---|---|
| Long ago | The **old kingdoms** of Ardmor. Makers were honoured as poets and smiths were: a maker sat near the king's hearth, and their word could shame a lord. Making had old laws, the oldest being *never make with what is alive*. | Holy wells, standing stones, the old tongue, guest-right, the habit of calling a maker "a warm-hand" in irony, because makers are always cold |
| ~120 years ago | **The Conquest.** A dynasty came over the sea with mailed horsemen and Latin clerks. It built roads, churches, castles and courts of assize. The old kings became vassals or ghosts. | The Crown, the Church as the realm's official faith, a court tongue and a country tongue |
| ~32 years ago | **The White Winter.** Makers in the Crown's service tried to make a wheat that would grow through snow, breaking the oldest law. The snow-wheat took the fields, then failed all at once. Three years of famine followed. | Mass graves under chapels; people who still will not eat white bread; a deep public fear of making |
| ~29 years ago | **The Assize of Rings.** Every maker must be counted, licensed and ringed: an iron band soldered shut on the third finger, blessed by a priest. Unlicensed making is a hanging matter. | Wardens on the roads; licensed makers sent wherever the Crown or a lord pays; hedge-makers hanged at crossroads |
| Now | Peace, bread, order. There has not been a famine since. Most people think the Assize was worth it. | — |

**[author only] The secret under the White Winter.** The snow-wheat was a royal commission; the King's council wanted a crop that would feed an army through a winter campaign. When it failed, the Crown blamed the makers and passed the Assize, and so turned its own disaster into its strongest law. Very few people living know this. Lucan may be one of them. Reveal it late, through documents and through what people refuse to say.

## 2a. The White Winter and the founding of the Empire (user, 2026-10-03; replaces every older account in this file)

- **Before the Empire there were the free towns**: walled, self-ruling, quarrelsome, no master but themselves.
- **The White Winter** came, and has never ended: snow that will not melt in spring, fields white into midsummer, and every year it creeps a little further and takes a little more. Its cause is not decided; do not explain it in play.
- **The founder** understood what the winter was for. Wherever the snow fell he followed it with grain wagons and spears; starving towns opened their gates for bread, and he wrote their names upon his map. Town by town the map filled, and he crowned himself Emperor. His house still rules.
- **The winter serves the Empire**: where the Empire's grain does not go, nothing grows. Its power rests on being the only answer to the winter. Anything that answers the winter otherwise is treason.
- **The Lee** is an ordinary village at the ragged edge of the map, where people stopped running when the winter took their towns. Too far out for the Empire to bother with. Strangers are met warily; soldiers have come before.
- **Register:** epic Western fantasy (Absolum, Hades, The Lord of the Rings), never Chinese-historical.

## 3. Making (magic)

**What it is.** A maker joins two or three real things into one that carries something of each. Flour and hearth-ash become bread that keeps a year. Lime and river water become mortar that sets in the rain. Rosewood and winter make a rose that never closes. It needs real materials and the maker's bare hands. It cannot make something from nothing.

**What it costs.** Every making takes a little warmth from the hands that do it. A maker who works often is cold to the touch, sleeps near fires, and wears gloves in summer. Old makers die cold. (This is why warmth and touch carry weight between the hero and others; see §7.)

**Seams.** A made thing keeps a *seam*, a faint flaw where its parts met: a line in the grain, a taste under the taste. A good maker hides the seam; a great one puts it somewhere beautiful. People who handle made things learn to feel for it.

**The old laws** (the Lee still keeps them; the Crown keeps only the first, and only on paper):
- Never make with what is alive.
- Never unmake what another maker made while they live.
- A maker owes the first making of a new house to its hearth.

**Three registers:**

| The Lee | The street | The law |
|---|---|---|
| maker; a making; a made thing | ringed man; a ringed job | licensed practitioner; a sanctioned working |
| hedge-maker (no ring), said without shame | hedge-maker, said with fear | unlicensed practitioner; a felony |

Gameplay: every recipe is a making; the shop sells made things.

## 4. The Crown, the law and the Church

- **The Crown** rules through sheriffs, reeves and travelling **assizes** (real courts that rode from shire to shire).
- **The Assize of Rings** is both the law and the body that enforces it. Its riders are formally *Wardens of the Assize*; everyone else says *Wardens*. They carry lamps at night by custom: the Crown's light on the roads.
- **The Rolls.** The Crown counts everything: land, mills, makers. Its surveyors write the great **Book of the Land**, modelled on the real Domesday Book (1086). Blackthorn Wood is the blank page in that book.
- **The Church.** The Crown's faith, with bells, saints, Latin mass and priests who bless the rings. Ardmor took to it, after its own fashion: the holy wells were given saints' names, and the saints there behave a great deal like the older powers they replaced. No god's name appears in the game; people say *God* and *the saints* the way medieval people did.
- **Coin.** The Crown's silver penny, called a *king's head*. In the Lee, coin is rare and slightly suspect; debts are kept in favours, salt and labour.

## 5. Blackthorn Wood and the Good People

Blackthorn is the tree at the edge of the Otherworld in Irish folklore. Its fruit, sloes, are bitter until the frost has touched them. On the Crown's maps the wood is blank; thirty years of surveyors went in, and none came back to fill the page. Strangers who enter are turned round by the mist and walk out where they came in.

The Lee calls the wood **the Thorn** and never names what lives in it. If pressed, people say **the Good People**, or **Themselves**, or nothing. The customs:

- No names in the wood.
- No looking back on the way out.
- Don't touch the stones.
- The first of the milk and the last of the harvest are left out.
- Rowan over the door.

**[author only]** The wood let the hero in. No one in the Lee has seen it do that for an outsider. Why it did so is the long mystery of the game. It is tied to what makers are, to why making costs warmth, and to the Lee's last maker, who walked into the stones and did not come back (§6).

## 6. The Lee

**Place.** A valley inside the Thorn, out of the wind, hence the name. About sixty people live there in stone-and-thatch houses around a green and a holy well (**Brigid's Well**: a saint's name over something older). Standing stones mark the north edge, where the Badger Track comes in. There is a mill, a smithy, a brewhouse and an empty workshop.

**How they live.** Oats, barley, sheep, goats, bees, hazel and sloes. Trade with the outside goes through one or two people who know the way through the mist and come back with salt, iron, needles and news. No Crown tax and no Crown priest reach the Lee. Mass is said, when it is said, by a lay reader from an old book.

**Guest-right** (after the real Irish laws of hospitality). A stranger who reaches a hearth is fed before anyone asks their business, and no one asks their name until they have eaten. Refusing hospitality shames the host; abusing it shames the guest for life.

**Naming.** Gaelic given names (Muireann, Fiach, Ciara, Donnchadh, Bríd, Rónán). The Lee knows a person first by their work: *Muireann the Brewer*, *the Woodward*. A full name follows the parent's: *Fiach son of Ciara*. Giving your name is a small act of trust, which is why game cards show the epithet until the player learns the name.

**Speech.** Among themselves they speak the old tongue; with an outsider, a plain country speech with old-tongue turns. Their sayings:
- *Out of the wind.* (a greeting: "you're welcome here")
- *What the Thorn keeps, it keeps.*
- *Bitter till the frost.* (said of people who soften slowly)
- *Feed him first.*

**Seasons.** **May Eve** (fires, cattle driven between them) and **Summer's End** (the year's door; the dead visit; no one goes into the Thorn). These are the plain English forms of Beltane and Samhain.

**The empty workshop.** It belonged to **Bríd**, the Lee's last maker, an old hedge-maker who never wore a ring. Three winters ago she walked into the stones and did not come back. Her tools are still on the bench. Because the Lee has had no maker since, there are a hundred small things that no longer get made. This is the shop the hero takes over.

## 6a. Beyond the Lee: the wider world

The game never leaves the Lee and the Thorn. The world outside still has to exist: places, roads, trades and quarrels that people mention in passing and keep mentioning. A name the player hears once is decoration. A name they hear five times, from five people who each feel differently about it, makes the world real.

### Map in words

Ardmor is a large, wet country with its back to the western ocean. The Crown came from **Valmont**, across the southern sea; Ardmor's people call the newcomers and their descendants **Southrons** (a real Scots word for southerners), never to their faces. East, across a narrower sea, are the trading towns of the **Easterlings** (the real medieval English name for Hanseatic merchants), who belong to no crown and sell to everyone.

The Thorn lies in the middle-west of Ardmor, between the river **Blackwater** to the south and the bare uplands people call **the Hungry Hills** to the north.

## 7. The hero

**User rulings 2026-10-03:** an ordinary man with no special power; crafting is gameplay only; no ring; say *the Empire*. He is wanted because **he has found a clue to a way to stand against the White Winter**, and he is fleeing to the edge of the Empire's reach. The opening says that much and no more.

Proposal, awaiting the user's confirmation (author only, not in the opening):
- About thirty-three. A knight in the Emperor's service, raised to it from a boy taken from a town the winter had broken.
- **The clue:** his company was sent to burn a hamlet that had hidden its seed corn. In the cellar he found what they were hiding: a sack of old grain from the free towns, with green shoots pushing up through frost on the cellar floor. Grain that grows in the winter. The Empire burns villages for hiding seed because some of that seed is this.
- He did not burn the hamlet. He took the sack and rode. The forty riders are his own company; the Empire wants him alive to learn where the rest of the seed is, and who else knows.
- At the Lee the sack is the secret he carries, and the first thing he will one day have to decide to plant or to hide.
